# Images and Credits

The photographs and illustrations throughout this project help tell the story of Voyager 1 and Voyager 2. Most of the mission imagery comes from NASA and JPL archives. Each image is documented here with its subject, original source and appropriate credit.

Every entry also says what kind of material the image is: a photograph taken by a Voyager spacecraft, a photograph of the spacecraft or its launch, an artist’s concept, a ground-based astronomical observation, a diagram, or a screenshot of an official NASA webpage. No camera has ever photographed a Voyager spacecraft in flight, so every picture of a Voyager travelling through space is an artist’s concept, not a photograph.

Credits follow the wording NASA uses on its own pages for each kind of material, and they differ from image to image. NASA/JPL, NASA/KSC, NASA/JPL-Caltech and Caltech/Palomar all appear below. Where an entry links to a gallery or an overview page instead of a single image page, the exact original asset page was not independently identified, and the link leads to the official NASA collection that presents the subject.

The rights in these images stay with their original owners. A credit is not permission to reuse an image. See [License.md](License.md) and [NASA’s media usage guidelines](https://www.nasa.gov/nasa-brand-center/images-and-media/).

## Voyager 1

From Jupiter and Saturn to the Pale Blue Dot, the heliopause and the one-light-day milestone.

### voyager1_jupiter.jpeg

An image from Voyager 1’s encounter with Jupiter, which reached its closest approach on March 5, 1979. The encounter found active volcanoes on Io, the first seen on another body in the Solar System. The image marks the Jupiter flyby on the Voyager 1 timeline.

Type: Photograph taken by Voyager 1

Source: [NASA Science, Voyager at Jupiter](https://science.nasa.gov/gallery/voyager-at-jupiter/)

Credit: NASA/JPL.

### voyager1_saturn.jpeg

An image from Voyager 1’s encounter with Saturn, which reached its closest approach on November 12, 1980. The close pass of the moon Titan bent the spacecraft’s path out of the plane of the planets and ended its tour of the planets. The image marks the Saturn flyby on the timeline.

Type: Photograph taken by Voyager 1

Source: [NASA Science, Voyager at Saturn](https://science.nasa.gov/gallery/voyager-at-saturn/)

Credit: NASA/JPL.

### voyager1_pale_blue_dot.jpeg

The famous Pale Blue Dot photograph captured by Voyager 1 on February 14, 1990. Earth appears as a tiny point of light within a band of scattered sunlight. It is one of the central photographs in this project.

Type: Photograph taken by Voyager 1

Source: [NASA, Voyager 1’s Pale Blue Dot](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/voyager/voyager-1s-pale-blue-dot/) and [NASA, The Pale Blue Dot Download](https://science.nasa.gov/resource/voyager-pale-blue-dot-download/)

Credit: NASA/JPL-Caltech.

### voyager1_family_portrait.jpeg

The Solar System Family Portrait, a mosaic made from the pictures Voyager 1 took on February 14, 1990, when it turned its cameras back towards the Sun and the planets. It was the first portrait of the Solar System seen from the outside, and the Pale Blue Dot belongs to the same series. These were the last images taken by either Voyager. The cameras were switched off afterwards to save power.

Type: Photograph taken by Voyager 1, shown as a mosaic

Source: [NASA, Voyager 1’s Pale Blue Dot](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/voyager/voyager-1s-pale-blue-dot/) and [NASA, 10 Things: Going Interstellar](https://science.nasa.gov/missions/voyager-program/10-things-going-interstellar/)

Credit: NASA/JPL-Caltech.

### voyager1_deep_space.jpeg

An illustration of Voyager 1 travelling through deep space. It accompanies the milestone of February 17, 1998, when Voyager 1 overtook Pioneer 10 and became the most distant human-made object.

Type: Artist’s concept

Source: [JPL, Voyager 1](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/missions/voyager-1/)

Credit: NASA/JPL-Caltech.

### voyager1_termination_shock.jpeg

An illustration of the edge of the Sun’s influence, where the solar wind slows abruptly at the termination shock. Voyager 1 crossed the termination shock on December 16, 2004. The picture is a concept, not a photograph.

Type: Artist’s concept

Source: [NASA Science, Voyagers in the Heliosheath (Artist Concept)](https://science.nasa.gov/photojournal/voyagers-in-the-heliosheath-artist-concept/)

Credit: NASA/JPL-Caltech.

### voyager1_interstellar_space.jpeg

An illustration of Voyager 1 entering interstellar space. On August 25, 2012, Voyager 1 crossed the heliopause and became the first human-made spacecraft to do so. Crossing the heliopause means entering interstellar space. It does not mean passing beyond every part of the Sun’s gravitational reach, because the Oort Cloud lies far beyond.

Type: Artist’s concept

Source: [NASA Science, What Is a Light-Day?](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/voyager/voyager-1/voyager-1-what-is-a-light-day/)

Credit: NASA/JPL-Caltech.

### voyager1_one_light_day.jpeg

An artist’s concept of Voyager 1 at one light-day from Earth, a distance of about 25.9 billion kilometres (16.1 billion miles) that it reached on November 18, 2026. It is the most recent milestone on the timeline. The spacecraft has not been photographed at this distance, so the picture is an illustration.

Type: Artist’s concept

Source: [NASA, NASA’s Voyager 1 Makes History Reaching One Light-Day From Earth](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasas-voyager-1-makes-history-reaching-one-light-day-from-earth/) and [NASA Science, What Is a Light-Day?](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/voyager/voyager-1/voyager-1-what-is-a-light-day/)

Credit: NASA/JPL-Caltech.

## Beyond the Solar System

These pictures illustrate projections and distant stars, not events that have already happened. None of them is a photograph of a future encounter.

### voyager_power_conservation.jpeg

An illustration of the power story behind the mission’s end. The power source on each spacecraft produces about 4 watts less electricity every year, so the team switches off instruments and heaters one by one. It appears with the timeline entry on the remaining years of communication, whose end NASA has estimated but not announced.

Type: Illustration

Source: [NASA Science, Voyager Frequently Asked Questions](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/voyager/frequently-asked-questions/)

Credit: NASA/JPL-Caltech.

### voyager2_one_light_day_future.jpeg

An illustration that accompanies the projected milestone for Voyager 2, which is travelling towards the same one-light-day distance that Voyager 1 reached in 2026. It is a concept, not a photograph, and the milestone itself has not yet happened.

Type: Artist’s concept

Source: [NASA Science, What Is a Light-Day?](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/voyager/voyager-1/voyager-1-what-is-a-light-day/)

Credit: NASA/JPL-Caltech.

### voyager1_one_light_year_future.jpeg

An illustration that accompanies the projection of Voyager 1 reaching a distance of one light-year from Earth. A light-day and a light-year are very different distances, and this is a mathematical projection, not a scheduled event. The picture is a concept, not a photograph.

Type: Artist’s concept

Source: [NASA Science, What Is a Light-Day?](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/voyager/voyager-1/voyager-1-what-is-a-light-day/)

Credit: NASA/JPL-Caltech.

### voyager1_gliese445_future.jpeg

A ground-based photograph of the star Gliese 445, also known as AC +79 3888, made with the Oschin Schmidt Telescope near San Diego on April 22, 1998. NASA says Voyager 1 will pass within about 1.7 light-years of this star in about 40,000 years, around the year 40,272. The picture shows the star as it appears from Earth. It was not taken by the spacecraft, and it is not an image of a future encounter.

Type: Astronomical observation

Source: [NASA Science, Heading toward Gliese 445](https://science.nasa.gov/photojournal/heading-toward-gliese-445) and [NASA Photojournal, PIA17461](https://photojournal.jpl.nasa.gov/catalog/PIA17461)

Credit: Caltech/Palomar. The telescope is operated by the California Institute of Technology and Palomar Observatory. NASA’s own image article for the annotated version, which circles the star in red, carries the credit NASA/JPL-Caltech.

### voyager2_ross248_future.jpeg

An artist’s concept that puts Voyager 2’s travels in perspective against the scale of the Solar System. NASA says that in about 40,000 years Voyager 2 will come within about 1.7 light-years of Ross 248, a small star in the constellation Andromeda. That is a stellar flyby at a great distance, not a visit. The picture is an illustration, not a photograph.

Type: Artist’s concept

Source: [NASA Science, Voyager 2 and the Scale of the Solar System (Artist’s Concept)](https://science.nasa.gov/photojournal/voyager-2-and-the-scale-of-the-solar-system-artists-concept)

Credit: NASA/JPL-Caltech.

## The Golden Record

Each Voyager spacecraft carries a 12-inch gold-plated copper phonograph record. It holds 115 pictures, spoken greetings in 55 languages, natural sounds, and about 90 minutes of music. Audio recordings and photographs are different kinds of material, and the images below should not be mistaken for the recordings they illustrate. No audio from the record is reproduced in this project, and the full set of 115 pictures is not reproduced either.

NASA explains that most of the material on the record was copyrighted by its creators and owners, and that the people who assembled the record obtained releases to use it. NASA publishes only the material for which releases were available. It also states that the pictures on the record are copyright protected and that reproducing them requires permission from the copyright holder. For this reason the entries below for the record’s pictures, greetings and music point to NASA’s pages and claim no rights in the material itself.

### golden_record_front.jpeg

The Voyager Golden Record, a 12-inch gold-plated copper phonograph record. Each spacecraft carries one, as a message from Earth to any advanced civilisation that might one day find it. It is the centrepiece of the Golden Record section.

Type: Photograph of the Golden Record

Source: [NASA Science, Golden Record Contents](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/voyager/golden-record-contents/)

Credit: NASA/JPL-Caltech.

### golden_record_cover.jpeg

The protective aluminium cover that encases each record, together with a cartridge and a needle. Symbolic instructions engraved on it explain the origin of the spacecraft and show how the record is to be played.

Type: Photograph of the record’s cover

Source: [NASA Science, The Golden Record Cover](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/voyager/golden-record-cover/)

Credit: NASA/JPL-Caltech.

### golden_record_instructions.jpeg

The playback instructions engraved on the cover, shown as diagrams. They show the record and its stylus, how long one turn of the record takes, how the recorded signal is meant to be turned back into pictures, and, with a map of pulsars, where the spacecraft came from.

Type: Diagram engraved on the record’s cover

Source: [NASA Science, The Golden Record Cover](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/voyager/golden-record-cover/)

Credit: NASA/JPL-Caltech.

### golden_record_images.jpeg

A single picture that stands for the 115 images encoded on the Golden Record. Those images show human anatomy, biology, mathematics, physics, daily life, architecture, nature and the diversity of Earth. This project does not reproduce the full set.

Type: Picture representing the Golden Record’s images

Source: [NASA Science, Images on the Golden Record](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/voyager/golden-record-contents/images/)

Credit: The original photographer or copyright holder named in NASA’s Golden Record image gallery. The rights remain with them. NASA states that the pictures on the record are copyright protected.

### golden_record_greetings.jpeg

A picture that stands for the spoken greetings on the Golden Record, which are given in 55 languages, from Akkadian, spoken in Sumer about six thousand years ago, to Wu, a modern Chinese dialect. The image is a visual only. The greetings are audio recordings, and none is reproduced in this project.

Type: Picture representing the Golden Record’s greetings

Source: [NASA Science, Golden Record Greetings](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/voyager/golden-record-contents/greetings/)

Credit: The original photographer or copyright holder named by NASA for the picture. The rights remain with them.

### golden_record_sounds.jpeg

A screenshot captured from the Sounds of Earth section of NASA’s official Golden Record Sounds and Music webpage, which lists the sounds placed on the record. The record holds natural sounds such as surf, wind, thunder, birds and whales and other animals. This image is a screenshot of a webpage. It is not an audio recording, and it is not a photograph supplied independently by NASA.

Type: Screenshot of an official NASA webpage

Source: [NASA Science, Golden Record Sounds and Music](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/voyager/golden-record-contents/sounds/)

Credit: Screenshot of a NASA Science webpage. The recordings listed on the page belong to their own rights holders.

### golden_record_music.jpeg

A picture that stands for the music on the Golden Record, an eclectic selection of about 90 minutes that includes Eastern and Western classics and a variety of ethnic music from different cultures and eras. The image is a visual only. No music from the record is reproduced in this project.

Type: Picture representing the Golden Record’s music

Source: [NASA Science, Golden Record Sounds and Music](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/voyager/golden-record-contents/sounds/)

Credit: The original photographer or copyright holder named by NASA for the picture. The rights remain with them. The music on the record belongs to its performers and rights holders.
